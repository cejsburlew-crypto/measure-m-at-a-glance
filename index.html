Where the premium comes from — and why larger numbers get quoted. Peer-reviewed delivery research puts the differences between delivery methods in the single digits on unit cost: Konchar & Sanvido’s 351-project CII study, and the later two-decade ASCE synthesis, both rank design-build ahead of CM-at-Risk and Design-Bid-Build by a few percent — not tens of percent. A Lease-Leaseback GMP then adds identifiable costs on top: preconstruction fees paid before a price exists, an owner-visible errors-and-omissions contingency, and the largest factor — loss of price competition at the prime level, since the GMP is negotiated with one pre-selected firm instead of won by the lowest responsible bidder. Claims of 30–40% premiums nearly always compare a loaded LLB total against an unloaded construction cost, which is not a delivery-method comparison at all. No rigorous public study isolates a California K-12 Lease-Leaseback premium, so these bands are anchored on Balfour Beatty’s own priced work for these three buildings rather than on published averages.

Why Lease-Leaseback exists, and what has changed
Lease-Leaseback was developed to improve collaboration, constructability and project planning by involving the contractor earlier in the design process. Historically this approach was believed to reduce costs, improve quality and accelerate delivery. However, modern construction practices — complete digital design, Building Information Modeling, advanced coordination software, standardised quality control and improved project management — have significantly reduced many of the challenges Lease-Leaseback was originally intended to solve.

Today both Lease-Leaseback and traditional Design-Bid-Build are capable of delivering high-quality facilities on schedule when properly managed. The primary difference is often how the project is procured and priced. Design-Bid-Build relies on competitive bidding after complete design documents are finalised, while Lease-Leaseback typically includes negotiated fees and separately identified indirect costs such as contractor fees, general conditions and other project overhead.

Current research has not demonstrated that Lease-Leaseback consistently delivers lower construction costs than Design-Bid-Build, and many modern projects indicate that project success is driven more by the quality of the design, contractor performance and project management than by the procurement method itself. Consistent with the delivery-method literature cited above: Konchar & Sanvido, 1998; Sullivan, El Asmar, Chalhoub & Obeid, 2017.

2
Risk loading is the variable that moves the number
Risk loading is the variable that moves the number. The question behind every Lease-Leaseback proposal is simply: how much uncertainty is each proposer asking the District to pay for? Unresolved scope, escalation, labor availability, subcontractor market conditions, and schedule risk all get priced in as contingency. That premium — not the physical building — is what carries a $600–750/SF project into the $900–1,050+/SF band.
3
The GMP proposal and its ± tolerance are the risk-loading disclosure
The GMP proposal and its ± tolerance are the risk-loading disclosure — read them together. A GMP proposal is submitted as a guaranteed maximum price with a stated ± tolerance around it, and that tolerance band is the range of risk the proposer is asking the District to carry. A tight tolerance says the proposer has resolved its scope and is standing behind the number; a wide tolerance says the uncertainty is still open and the District is being asked to fund the high end of it. Two proposals with the same headline GMP are not equivalent if one carries ±3% and the other ±15% — the second is a materially more expensive proposal with the difference hidden in the band rather than the price.

A GMP ROM is priced by that contractor’s subcontractor pool, not by the market. A Rough Order of Magnitude estimate reflects the experience and the specific subs that this general contractor brings, and the same scope bid through a different GC returns different numbers: subs load their bids differently depending on who they are working for — relationship, payment history, backlog, and administrative burden all move the price. The same subcontractor routinely bids one GC materially differently than another. So the ROM and its tolerance together tell you how well that GC knows its pool and how much risk that pool is pricing in. A wide tolerance on a ROM signals an unsettled pool and hands that uncertainty to the District; a tighter tolerance around a lower range signals a contractor whose subs are known, competitive, and already accounted for — less risk and a lower likely landing point.
4
Do not confuse the ± tolerance with the GMP monthly cost
Do not confuse the ± tolerance with the GMP monthly cost. They answer different questions. The monthly cost is the pay-application draw against the GMP as work is completed — that is cash flow and timing. The ± tolerance is exposure: how far the final number can move against the District. A favorable monthly billing rate tells you nothing about how much risk premium is buried in the GMP, and a low draw early in the job does not shrink the tolerance band. Evaluate them separately — tolerance for how much uncertainty the District is buying, monthly cost for when the District pays for it.
5
What this does to FMP buying power
What this does to FMP buying power. At the midpoints — $675/SF DBB versus $975/SF heavily loaded Lease-Leaseback — the same dollar buys roughly 30% less scope. An FMP tier sized to deliver a full scope list under Design-Bid-Build should be read as delivering about two-thirds of that list if the work goes out as heavily risk-loaded Lease-Leaseback.

Evaluating True Project Cost Lease-Leaseback proposal checklist — what actually moves the final number …

1
Normalize every proposal to one number, not a fee percentage. Fee is the smallest lever on the page. Rebuild each proposal as precon + (monthly GC × a realistic duration) + fee% + bond% + liability% + contingency% + allowances, applied to the same cost of work. A lower fee with a higher monthly GC or a longer assumed schedule loses.
2
Duration is a price term, not a schedule note. At roughly $100K per month, every added month costs about $100K in general conditions alone — and the 12-month assumption here is explicitly “to be revised during precon.” Lock the monthly rate and settle now who absorbs GC’s when DSA review, phasing, or District-caused delay extends the job.
3
Ask who keeps unused contingency and savings. A 3% errors-and-omissions contingency on a $20M cost of work is $600K. Does it return to the District at closeout or convert to contractor profit? Explicit savings-return language is worth more than a point of fee.
4
Require open-book subcontractor bidding. Because the ROM is only as good as the sub pool, insist on a minimum number of bids per trade, District presence at bid openings and leveling, and disclosure of any scope the GC intends to self-perform — self-performed work should be competitively tested, not negotiated.
5
Separate allowances, contingency and escalation. Three different risks. Escalation should carry a stated date basis. Confirm the same uncertainty is not priced once in escalation, again in contingency, and a third time inside the sub bids.
6
Check whether markups stack on markups. Verify fee is not calculated on contingency, that bond and insurance are not applied to fee, and that allowances sit outside the percentage base. Stacked markups quietly add several percent to the total.
7
Understand SDI versus subcontractor bonds. Subcontractor Default Insurance in lieu of sub bonds (1.25% here) protects and is controlled by the contractor; a payment and performance bond gives the District a surety to call. Cheaper is not the same as safer.
8
Fix change-order mechanics before the GMP. Lock labor and equipment unit rates, cap change-order markup, and define “unforeseen conditions” in writing. Post-GMP growth is where LLB projects drift, and most of it traces to unresolved scope at GMP establishment.
9
Tie preconstruction fees to deliverables. Precon dollars are paid before a GMP exists. Attach them to defined products — estimates at DD and CD, a constructability log, subcontractor outreach evidence — so value is judged before the large money commits.
10
Compare $/GSF on identical scope definitions. Confirm gross-square-foot definitions match and that site work, utilities and the perimeter rule (five feet beyond the building at Carver, for example) are treated the same in every proposal. Otherwise the per-square-foot comparison is meaningless.

Author’s derivations — not sourced

Every figure below is the author’s own construction, not a published number. Each is flagged inline wherever it appears on this page. They are reasoning aids for framing questions to Balfour Beatty and to the board; none should be quoted as source data, and none should be relied on without testing the input assumption behind it.

The 12.5% and 19% escalation rates. Both carry the FMP’s January 2024 basis forward — 12.5% to 2026 pricing and 19% to a 2027 construction window — at roughly 6% a year compounded. No ENR, RSMeans, Caltrans or DGS index was used, and neither the FMP nor Balfour’s proposal states an escalation basis. Every escalation-adjusted variance on this page moves directly with these two numbers.

All six delivery-method bands. The three new-construction bands ($820–910, $910–1,000, $1,000–1,120/SF) and the three modernization bands ($270–320, $320–370, $370–430/SF). No rigorous public study isolates a California K-12 lease-leaseback premium, so the Design-Bid-Build band is anchored on the FMP’s own rates and the two lease-leaseback bands are spaced above it using Balfour’s own loaded pricing as the upper reference. The “% vs DBB midpoint” premiums are arithmetic on boundaries the author chose.

The $25/SF abatement and $35/SF interface-demolition allowances on the Career Center. Neither appears in any source document. There is no hazardous-materials survey for Building P in hand and no demolition take-off for the tie-in to the new building; both are placeholders for scope that is real but unquantified.

The 22% load applied to the Career Center. Not a Balfour rate for that line, which carries no markup at all. It is the average markup ratio observed across their other three projects, applied so the renovation can be compared on a loaded basis.

The 19.70% / 14.42% / 13.19% cost-scaling markup rates, derived by subtraction. Balfour discloses one markup subtotal per project and nothing inside it; each rate is the residual after removing the general conditions, preconstruction and allowances they do state, divided by direct cost. The method assumes the disclosed general-conditions totals are exactly what sits inside the markup pool, which cannot be verified. After also removing the stated 8.45% percentage loading (fee 3.25% + bond 1.00% + general liability 1.20% + contingency 3.00%), $4,285,661 of the $12,970,468 total markup pool remains unreconciled to any stated component across the three projects. These are residuals, not a fee schedule.

The micro / small / medium GC tier $/SF ranges and the bonding brackets. Built by scaling the staffing and home-office overhead in Balfour’s own General Conditions sheets on the assumption that a leaner team drops the part-time BIM, scheduling and operations-support roles and carries lower fee and overhead. The statutory revenue thresholds are sourced (DGS, 2022; SBA, 2023; Cal. Pub. Cont. Code); the $/SF ranges and bonding brackets attached to each tier are not. They are planning ranges, not quotes, and only the large-GC row reflects an actual proposal.

A ×1.33 soft-cost load applied to normalized construction. RETIRED — no longer used anywhere on this page. An earlier version of the budget comparison reconstructed each project’s soft costs by multiplying normalized construction by the FMP’s 1.33 factor. That was unnecessary and wrong in method: the District’s budget itemises soft costs as their own line, so there was nothing to reconstruct. The comparison now runs against the LLB BB Construction budget line directly. Listed here rather than deleted so the change is auditable.

The 20,993 sf versus 14,200 sf denominator choice in the modernize-or-build-new comparison. The 20,993 sf program area comes from the Huckabee document and appears nowhere in the FMP export; the FMP’s own HS13 line carries three different and overlapping treatment areas (14,200 / 6,034 / 10,860 sf) and states no total wing area. The choice of denominator moves the modernization rate between $600/sf and $886/sf — nearly $300/sf — and must be resolved before either rate is used publicly.
